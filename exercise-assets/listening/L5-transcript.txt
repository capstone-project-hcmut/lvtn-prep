LECTURER: Good morning. Today I want to look at one of the oldest pieces of water engineering still in use anywhere in the world: the qanat. That's spelt Q, A, N, A, T. Qanats were first built in what is now Iran, probably around three thousand years ago, and the idea later spread across the Middle East, into North Africa, and even as far as Spain.

LECTURER: The problem a qanat solves is a simple one. In a dry region, there is often water underground at the foot of the mountains, but the villages and fields are some distance away, out on the plain. A qanat is essentially a tunnel that carries that underground water to where it's needed, using nothing but gravity.

LECTURER: Let me describe how it's laid out, and you can follow on the diagram in your handout. At the upper end, near the hills, is the deepest shaft, which is known as the mother well. This is dug down until it reaches the water-bearing layer, which in most cases is a layer of gravel lying underneath the foothills.

LECTURER: From the bottom of the mother well, a tunnel runs out towards the plain, sloping very gently downwards. Along the route of the tunnel, there's a line of vertical shafts. These were used to remove the soil during digging, but they also provide ventilation for the workers underground, and later, access for repairs.

LECTURER: Where the ground is soft, the tunnel is lined with oval hoops made of baked clay, to stop it from collapsing. And at the lower end, the tunnel reaches the surface, and the water flows out into an open channel, and on to the fields.

LECTURER: So how was one built? The first step was finding the water. The surveyor would look carefully at the base of the hills for vegetation. A line of green plants in otherwise dry ground was the best sign that there was water underneath.

LECTURER: Next, the mother well was dug. But the builders didn't commit straight away. They would monitor the well for a full year, including a dry summer, to be sure that the flow was reliable. Only then did the main work begin.

LECTURER: And here's something that surprises most people. You might expect them to start digging the tunnel from the mother well and work downhill. In fact, they started at the outlet end, out on the plain, and dug uphill, towards the mother well. The reason is safety. If you start at the top and hit water, the tunnel floods with you inside it. Starting from the bottom, any water simply drains away behind you.

LECTURER: Keeping the tunnel straight underground was another challenge. The diggers did it by hanging lamps in two neighbouring shafts, and lining them up by eye. And the slope itself had to be very shallow. If the water ran too fast, it would cause erosion of the tunnel walls, and eventually, the tunnel would collapse.

LECTURER: Why go to all this trouble, instead of simply digging a canal? The main advantage is that, because the water travels underground, very little of it is lost to evaporation. In a hot climate, that loss from an open canal is enormous.

LECTURER: Sadly, many qanats have been abandoned over the last century. From the nineteen-sixties onwards, deep wells with diesel pumps were drilled across the region. They lowered the water table, and a great many qanats simply ran dry.

LECTURER: But there has been a revival of interest. In twenty sixteen, eleven Iranian qanats were added to UNESCO's World Heritage list, and a number of communities are now restoring their old tunnels, and in some cases, bringing them back into use.
